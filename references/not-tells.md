# Not tells

Every flag spends the writer's attention. Spend it on things that are actually signals, because an audit that cries wolf gets switched off, and then the real findings go unread too.

This file is the counterweight to the catalogue: patterns that get called AI tells and are not, on their own.

## Punctuation and mechanics

**The em dash as a character.** The strongest claim in circulation and the weakest in practice. Writers have used the dash for three centuries. What separates machine text is the *rate*, around one every two or three sentences, and the *splice habit* of joining two independent clauses with it. One dash on a page is punctuation. In Russian the dash between subject and predicate is mandatory, and in German the spaced Gedankenstrich is ordinary; applying an English rate limit there is wrong twice.

**Semicolons.** Sometimes called a tell because models use them correctly. Correct use is not evidence of anything.

**Curly quotes and apostrophes.** Word, Google Docs, macOS and iOS all curl them by default. Meaningful only in plain-text contexts where nothing auto-curls, and never on their own.

**Clean grammar, consistent spacing, no typos.** Careful writers exist. So do editors, spellcheckers and second drafts. The inverse is worth more: when editing someone's rough casual text, leave their typos alone, because those are the fingerprint.

## Register and vocabulary

**A single tier-1 word.** One "delve" is a word that has been in English since the ninth century. Four of them in a page, next to "robust" and "seamless", is a signal. Judge the cluster, never the token.

**Formal register and long words.** This is what academic, legal and second-language writing looks like. Detector audits report false-positive rates above 60% on non-native English writers precisely here. Flagging formality punishes the writer for not being a native speaker.

**"However", "moreover", "therefore".** A well-placed connective is good writing. A stack of them opening four paragraphs in a row is the tell.

**Passive voice in a method section.** Convention in scientific writing, and correct when the actor is unknown or irrelevant. The tell is the agentless passive that hides a responsible party.

## Shape

**Lists.** Content that is genuinely list-shaped belongs in a list: parameters, steps, options. The tell is bullets of bare noun phrases standing in for prose that was never written.

**Headings and structure.** A long document needs navigation. The tell is a heading every two paragraphs on a 400-word page, or a summary of a text short enough to read.

**Short sentences.** Brevity is not slop. Manufactured staccato is: sentences chopped to fake a rhythm the content did not earn.

**Emoji.** In chat and social posts they are the register. In a heading of a technical document they are a tell.

## What to do instead

When something feels off but lands on this page, the finding is usually one level up: not the word but its density, not the dash but the rate, not the list but what the list replaced. Say that instead, or say nothing.

And when the writer chose the flagged thing on purpose, mark the line `prose-ignore` and stop asking. A skill that keeps re-raising a settled decision is training the writer to skip its output.

## Where this came from

The principle and the discipline of a "cleared, do not chase" list are borrowed from [unslop-ui](https://github.com/JCarterJohnson/vibecoded-design-tells) by Carter Johnson (MIT), which ranks visual tells against a Reddit corpus and publishes what the data cleared: mesh backgrounds, bento grids, dark mode itself, shadcn and Tailwind as tools.

One difference worth stating plainly. Their ranking rests on a measured corpus. **This file does not.** It is editorial judgement about prose, and the entries here are argued, not counted. Treat the ordering as opinion until somebody measures it.
